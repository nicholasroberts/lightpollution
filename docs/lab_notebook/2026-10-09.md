## Opening summary

Today's work materially changed the structure of the future-prediction problem.

The starting empirical result remains very strong: the historical increase in corrected VIIRS radiance is highly density-dependent. Across 2013–2024, low-density NUTS3 regions brightened at about **2% yr⁻¹**, whereas growth was much weaker at moderate density and statistically indistinguishable from zero in the densest class. This means that a single Europe-wide ALAN growth rate is not an adequate description of the data.

A five-year change analysis on fixed NUTS2016 geography then showed that **population-density change itself adds only modest explanatory power** once baseline density, country, and time window are represented. Even when population density is held approximately constant, the historical data still predict positive radiance growth. Population redistribution is therefore relevant, but it is not sufficient by itself to explain where radiance grows.

This motivated a new static physical-geography variable, **terrain-constrained habitable fraction (H)**, defined independently of population and VIIRS. (H) is the fraction of a NUTS3 polygon physically available for habitation under explicit elevation, slope, ruggedness, permanent-water, and permanent-snow/ice constraints. The metric behaves geographically as intended: flat lowland NUTS3 regions approach (H=1), whereas Alpine regions can have (H<0.1).

The first attempt to use (H) as a simple correction to population density,
[
D_H = D/H,
]
was a useful negative result: it **substantially worsened** the continuous population–radiance model. Terrain-adjusted density should therefore not replace ordinary population density.

However, (H) is not irrelevant. Direct matched VIIRS observations show that among nominally sparse NUTS3 regions (<100 people km⁻²), low-(H) regions experienced much larger 2013–2020 radiance increases than high-(H) regions. More stringent models show that the important structure is **density-dependent**: adding (H) alone contributes essentially nothing after headroom controls, but the block containing (H) and its interaction with baseline population density is strongly supported. The emerging model is therefore not (D/H), and not (D+H) with one universal additive (H) effect, but a response surface in which terrain habitability modifies what a given population density means for subsequent radiance change.

The current working forecasting structure is therefore:

[
g_R = f(D,\Delta D,H,D\times H),
]

with baseline radiance/headroom and country/technology effects treated separately as required. The intended future output remains **percentage change in VIIRS-equivalent night-time radiance relative to the present**, not absolute future radiance.

The most important unresolved issue for the next session is to combine the density, population-density-change, and (H) terms in one historically validated model, preferably first on fixed NUTS2016 geography, and then test predictive performance rather than relying only on significance tests.

## 1. Scientific objective at the start of the session

The long-term aim is to use the historical relationship between human population and VIIRS night-time radiance to predict future changes in the nocturnal light environment.

The project is **not** intended simply to report a log–log correlation between population density and light pollution. The two central objectives are:

1. use the historical population–radiance relationship to predict future **percentage changes** in night-time radiance under projected demographic change; and
2. ultimately combine those radiance changes with a separate relationship between radiance and a biologically relevant visual cue, allowing future visibility of that cue to be projected.

A central difficulty is that low population density does not mean the same thing everywhere. A sparsely populated agricultural region of Spain may still contain large areas available for settlement and infrastructure, whereas the nominally sparse area surrounding Mont Blanc contains very little land physically available for habitation. This motivated the development of (H).

## 4. Population-density change as a predictor of radiance change

### Motivation

A naive forecast would be to take the historical ~2% yr⁻¹ low-density growth rate and apply it to all future low-density regions. That is clearly unrealistic: an unchanging high-Alpine landscape should not automatically be assigned the same future growth as a developable rural region.

The first attempted refinement was to use **population-density change** directly.

On a fixed NUTS polygon, percentage population change and percentage population-density change are identical. Density terminology was retained because it matches the rest of the model and future spatial population products.

### Analysis

Pipeline stage:

```bash
python src/run_pipeline.py density-demographic-surface
```

To avoid crossing NUTS boundary releases, this first pass used fixed NUTS2016 geography and overlapping five-year windows:

- 2013–2018
- 2014–2019
- 2015–2020

Response:

[
g_R=
\frac{\log_{10}(R_{end})-\log_{10}(R_{start})}{5}
]

Population-density change:

[
100\left[\left(P_{end}/P_{start}\right)^{1/5}-1\right]
]

The model included baseline density, population-density change, curvature/interactions, country and time-window effects. Covariance was clustered by NUTS_ID because the five-year windows overlap.

### Result

The demographic-change block was statistically supported:

```text
Wald chi2 = 16.234
df = 4
p = 0.00272
```

but incremental explanatory power was small:

```text
density-only R2 = 0.531997
density + demographic-change R2 = 0.534504
delta R2 = 0.002507
```

AIC modestly favoured the demographic surface, whereas BIC favoured the simpler density-only formulation.

The zero-population-density-change slice remained positive across the reference density range. For example:

| Density | Predicted radiance change with Δdensity = 0 |
|---:|---:|
| 10 km⁻² | +1.40% yr⁻¹ |
| 30 | +1.44% yr⁻¹ |
| 50 | +1.50% yr⁻¹ |
| 100 | +1.60% yr⁻¹ |
| 300 | +1.69% yr⁻¹ |
| 1000 | +1.55% yr⁻¹ |
| 3000 | +1.03% yr⁻¹ |

### Interpretation

Population-density change contributes information, but **contemporaneous NUTS3 demographic change does not explain most historical radiance growth**.

This is a descriptive statement at NUTS3 scale, not a causal claim and not evidence that population is irrelevant at finer spatial scales.

### Important unresolved discrepancy

The 2013–2020 five-year surface gives positive zero-demographic-change growth even at high density, whereas the full 2013–2024 temporal analysis shows little or no growth at high density.

Possible explanation: growth became increasingly concentrated in lower-density landscapes later in the record.

A matched-period density-band test remains worth doing explicitly.

## 5. Construction of the static terrain-habitability metric H

### Rationale

Mean NUTS3 population density uses total polygon area as the denominator, even when much of that land cannot realistically support settlement.

Therefore define:

[
H_i=
\frac{A_{physically\ habitable,i}}
{A_{total,i}}
]

where (H=1) means almost the whole NUTS3 is physically available for habitation and low (H) indicates strong terrain constraint.

Importantly, (H) was designed to be **temporally independent** and **anthropogenically independent**.

It deliberately excludes:

- current population;
- built-up fraction;
- roads;
- GDP;
- VIIRS radiance;
- future population;
- protected-area status.

This prevents the metric from learning the historical VIIRS result by construction.

### Inputs

- Copernicus DEM GLO-90
- DEM-derived slope
- 3×3 local elevation SD as a ruggedness measure
- Copernicus permanent-water cover fraction
- Copernicus permanent-snow/ice cover fraction

### Scenarios

Three threshold scenarios were retained as sensitivity analyses:

| Scenario | Max slope | Max elevation | Max local elevation SD |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| strict | 10° | 1500 m | 30 m |
| **core** | **15°** | **2000 m** | **50 m** |
| permissive | 20° | 2500 m | 75 m |

The thresholds are explicit modelling assumptions, not universal physiological definitions of habitability.

### Pipeline

```bash
python src/run_pipeline.py habitability-fetch
python src/run_pipeline.py habitability-build
```

Main outputs:


## 6. First historical validation: using D/H as terrain-adjusted density

### Hypothesis tested

A first idea was that nominal population density could be corrected for physically habitable area:

[
D_H = D/H
]

This would treat an Alpine NUTS3 with a small habitable fraction as effectively denser than a flat region with the same nominal population density.

### Direct low-density result

Within nominal-density regions <100 people km⁻², the initial mixed temporal analysis found:

| H tertile | Estimated radiance trend |
|---|---:|
| low H | +3.36% yr⁻¹ |
| mid H | +1.35% yr⁻¹ |
| high H | +1.00% yr⁻¹ |

Year × H-tertile:

```text
LR = 179.892
df = 2
p = 8.65e-40
```

The broader H modifier test was also very strong:

```text
LR = 226.701
df = 4
p = 6.77e-48
```

### Negative result: D/H is not a good replacement for D

Despite the striking temporal signal, simply replacing population density with (D/H) worsened the continuous population–radiance model substantially.

Core example:

```text
nominal density:
    AIC ≈ -42201
    marginal R2 ≈ 0.9149

terrain-adjusted core density:
    AIC ≈ -40016
    marginal R2 ≈ 0.8562
```

Strict and permissive variants showed the same qualitative outcome.

### Decision

**Do not use (D/H) as the main population-density variable.**

This is exactly the sort of negative result that should remain in the lab notebook even if it never appears in the final paper.

The result suggested instead that (H) should be treated as a separate physical-geography variable whose effect may depend on population density.

The formal mixed model was:

```text
log10(corrected radiance)
~ country x year
+ cubic nominal population density x year
+ H
+ H x year
+ (1 | NUTS_ID::NUTS_release)
```

This means that the H × year term is tested only after allowing:

- country-specific temporal trends;
- a nonlinear nominal-density × time surface;
- a static H main effect;

#### Results

| H definition | Period | H × year p |
|---|---|---:|
| strict | 2013–2024 | 0.158 |
| strict | 2013–2020 NUTS2016 | 0.109 |
| **core** | **2013–2024** | **0.0172** |

### 7.3 Temporally separated headroom test

To reduce the possibility that the effect is merely regression-to-the-mean or low-baseline headroom, baseline and outcome periods were separated.

Baseline predictors:

```text
mean 2013-2014 log radiance
mean 2013-2014 log population density
```

Outcome:

```text
2015-2020 NUTS3 log-radiance slope
```

Additional control:

```text
2015-2020 population-density slope
country
```

#### Model comparison

```text
reduced:
    R2 = 0.678308
    AIC = -8351.98

+ H only:
    R2 = 0.678357
    AIC = -8350.18
    H p = 0.691

+ H and H x baseline density:
    R2 = 0.687528
    AIC = -8385.64
```

For the joint H block:

```text
Wald chi2 = 25.493
df = 2
p = 2.91e-06

delta R2, H only = 0.000049
delta R2, H + density interaction = 0.009220
```

### Interpretation

This is probably the most important modelling result of the day.

- (H) alone contributes essentially nothing after baseline density, baseline radiance, demographic trend and country are controlled.
- The **density-dependent H block is strongly supported**.
- Therefore (H) should not be treated as a universal additive effect.
- The data support the idea that physical terrain constraint modifies how population density relates to subsequent radiance growth.

This reconciles the apparently conflicting direct and adjusted analyses: low-H regions can show rapid observed growth at the sparse end while a single Europe-wide H coefficient gives an incomplete or misleading summary.

## 8. Current working conceptual model

The historical evidence now points toward:

[
g_R =
f(D,\Delta D,H,D\times H)
]

where:

- (g_R) = historical/future log-radiance growth;
- (D) = baseline nominal population density;
- (Delta D) = population-density trajectory;
- (H) = static terrain-constrained habitable fraction;
- (D\times H) = the crucial interaction indicating that terrain means different things at different population densities.

Other terms remain conceptually separate:

- baseline radiance/headroom;
- country/policy/infrastructure residual structure;
- bounded spectral/LED correction;
- eventual ecological/visual-cue response.

The future product should remain:

> **projected percentage change in VIIRS-equivalent night-time radiance relative to 2024**

rather than an absolute VIIRS-radiance forecast.

**Status:** probably discard as a primary modelling strategy.

**Status:** not supported.

Value retained: this negative result identifies the interaction with density as the important structure.

### Population-density change alone as the missing predictor

**Status:** insufficient on its own.

The demographic-change block is statistically detectable but explains little additional variance ((Delta R^2\approx0.0025)).

Value retained: future population trajectories still matter, but should enter a richer model rather than replace the historical density-growth relationship.

### Country heterogeneity explained by density composition

**Status:** only partially supported.

The common density-time surface explains little of the country × year structure and only ~9% of country × density structure.

Value retained: country-level residual processes cannot currently be ignored.

## 10. Methodological cautions carried forward

### NUTS geography

The current full historical record uses:

- 2013–2020: NUTS2016
- 2021–2022: NUTS2021
- 2023–2024: NUTS2024

Change calculations should not silently cross boundary releases.

Where possible:

- use fixed NUTS2016 for historical mechanism tests;
- harmonise to a common geography before a production 2013–2024 change model.

### Calibration correction

Five countries have the additive dark-region correction from 2017:

```text
DE IT NL FR ES
```

The direct H analysis therefore reports:

- corrected VIIRS;
- raw VIIRS where available;
- a sensitivity analysis excluding calibration countries.

The low-H pattern persists in those sensitivity checks.

### Percentage change and baseline headroom

Low starting radiance can create large percentage changes.

Therefore:

- retain absolute change summaries;
- include lagged/baseline radiance as a predictor;
- avoid using baseline radiance simultaneously in ways that create mathematical coupling;
- prefer temporally separated predictor/outcome periods when testing headroom.

### Overlapping windows

The five-year demographic windows overlap. Cluster-robust covariance by NUTS_ID is used, but effective independent information is closer to the number of regions than the number of windows.

### H thresholds

Strict/core/permissive H definitions are sensitivity scenarios, not claims that 15° slope, 2000 m elevation, etc. are universal habitation limits.

The best definition should ultimately be judged by robustness and historical predictive value.

### Causality

These are observational models. Terms such as "predicts", "is associated with", and "explains statistical variation" are appropriate. Strong causal language is not yet justified.

## 14. Working narrative at end of day

A possible emerging scientific narrative is:

> Population density strongly predicts the spatial distribution of European night-time radiance, but the temporal dynamics are not spatially uniform. Since 2013, corrected VIIRS radiance has increased most rapidly in low-density landscapes, while densely populated regions show little net growth. Demographic change explains only a small additional fraction of that variation. A static terrain-habitability metric shows that physical geography further modifies the relationship: terrain constraint does not simply rescale population density, but interacts with it in predicting where radiance changes. This provides a physically interpretable route from historical observations to future spatial projections of percentage change in VIIRS-equivalent night-time radiance.

This is **not yet a final-paper conclusion**. It is the current working synthesis to be tested by the next round of joint modelling and cross-validation.

## CRITICAL ADDENDUM — 2017 VIIRS calibration scope

**Added after the main 2026-10-09 note was written.**

A major pipeline error was identified in the interpretation of the 2017 VIIRS
DNB calibration discontinuity.

The current production calibration code applies the post-2017 additive
correction only to DE, IT, NL, FR and ES. Those five countries entered the
pipeline because they were the original **comparison subset used to diagnose**
the discontinuity. They are not the physical domain of the calibration change.

The calibration change is a property of the VIIRS DNB data stream and therefore
affects the dataset globally. EOG explicitly documents a brightness jump in
2017 due to a VIIRS DNB calibration change. NOAA calibration work describes the
transition from airglow-contaminated dark-offset estimation before 2017 to a
deep-space-based dark-offset calibration from January 2017. Published work on
EOG DNB time series has used a 0.15 nW cm-2 sr-1 post-2017 subtraction as a
simple correction for this zero-point shift.

**Consequences:** all temporal results in this notebook that use
`radiance_mean_corrected` across 2017 must currently be treated as
**provisional**, including the density-dependent growth percentages, rotation,
demographic-change tests, and H historical tests. The raw extraction is
preserved and does not need to be repeated.

A new diagnostic stage,
`global-calibration-rotation-test`, has been added before replacing the
production calibration. It compares:

1. raw/unadjusted radiance;
2. the legacy five-country correction;
3. a global 0.15 nW cm-2 sr-1 correction;
4. a Europe-wide empirical dark-region offset;
5. a global offset equal to the median of independently estimated
   country-level dark-region offsets.

For each correction it recalculates the population-density growth percentages
and formal rotation tests over both 2013-2024 and fixed NUTS2016 geography
(2013-2020).

This addendum deliberately does not delete or rewrite the earlier results: they
remain part of the research record, but their calibration status is now
explicitly flagged.
